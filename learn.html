01 / SIGNS OF ABUSE

Recognising abusive behaviour

Abuse can be physical, emotional, sexual or financial. Some warning signs can be subtle and may become more controlling over time. Being aware of these patterns is the first step towards safety.

Emotional & psychological

  • Repeated insults, humiliation or name-calling.
  • Threatening harm to you, your children or loved ones.
  • Blaming you for the abuser's behaviour.
  • Gaslighting — making you doubt your own memory or perception.
  • Public embarrassment or constant criticism.

Controlling behaviour

  • Controlling who you see, where you go or what you wear.
  • Demanding access to your phone, messages or accounts.
  • Isolating you from friends and family.
  • Monitoring your movements or using tracking technology.
  • Making decisions on your behalf without consent.

Physical harm

  • Hitting, slapping, pushing, choking or kicking.
  • Threatening physical harm.
  • Destroying property or objects to intimidate you.
  • Blocking your exit or physically restraining you.
  • Using weapons or objects to threaten or cause harm.

Sexual & financial

  • Sexual activity without freely given consent.
  • Pressure, coercion or threats to obtain sexual activity.
  • Controlling access to money or basic needs.
  • Preventing you from working or studying.
  • Taking money or running up debt in your name.

If you recognise something concerning, you do not have to handle it alone. Consider speaking to someone you trust or a relevant support service. View resources →

02 / KNOW YOUR RIGHTS

You have the right
to be safe.

Everyone deserves dignity, bodily autonomy and safety. Understanding your rights can help you recognise when those rights are being violated and give you the confidence to seek support.

01

The right to dignity

Every person deserves to be treated with respect regardless of their relationship status, gender, age or background. No one has the right to dehumanise, humiliate or degrade you.

02

The right to bodily autonomy & consent

Consent must be freely given, informed and can be withdrawn at any time. Being in a relationship, being married or having consented before does not remove your right to say no. Silence or fear is not consent.

03

The right to seek help

You are allowed to ask for help, report abuse and access support services. No one should prevent you from reaching out to police, health services, shelters or legal assistance.

04

The right to privacy

You choose who you share your story with. Seeking help is a private matter and you are not obligated to disclose your situation to anyone you do not trust.

05

The right to set boundaries

Being in a relationship does not remove your right to personal limits — physical, emotional, sexual or financial. Healthy relationships are built on mutual respect for boundaries.

This is general awareness information only, not legal advice. For legal support contact a relevant organisation in your area. Find support →

03 / SAFER EXIT STRATEGIES

Planning for
a safer path.

Leaving an unsafe situation can carry serious risks. There is no single correct plan — every situation is different. These steps are intended to help you think ahead where possible.

Identify a safer place

Think about where you could go quickly if you needed to leave — a trusted friend or family member's home, a shelter, or a community organisation. Have an address or contact in mind.

Tell someone you trust

If it is safe to do so, let a trusted person know what is happening. They can be a point of contact, help with logistics or simply be aware of your situation.

Keep important items accessible

Where possible, keep documents such as your ID, passport and bank cards somewhere accessible — or with a trusted person outside the home. A small bag with essentials can help you leave quickly if needed.

Think about how to contact help safely

Consider how you can reach emergency services or a support line without increasing risk. If your phone is being monitored, use a friend's device, a public phone, or a private browser.

Digital safety

If technology is being used to monitor you, consider using a safer device or clearing your browsing history after visiting support sites. Most browsers allow private or incognito browsing.

After leaving

Leaving is not always the end of the risk. Consider changing contact details, informing schools or workplaces if needed, and seeking ongoing support from an organisation.

If you are in immediate danger, prioritise getting to a safer place and contacting emergency help. View emergency options →

04 / SUPPORTING SOMEONE

You don't have to be
an expert to help.

If someone you care about is experiencing abuse, your support can matter enormously. You do not need special training — listening without judgement is often the most powerful thing you can do.

What to do

  • Listen and take what they say seriously.
  • Ask what kind of support they want — don't assume.
  • Let them know you believe them.
  • Respect their choices, even if you disagree with them.
  • Help them find appropriate services if they want that support.
  • Check in on them regularly.
  • Keep what they share with you confidential.

What to avoid

  • Blaming or questioning why they stayed.
  • Minimising what happened ("it wasn't that bad").
  • Telling them what to do or pressuring them to leave.
  • Confronting the alleged abuser yourself — this can increase danger.
  • Sharing their story without permission.
  • Making promises you cannot keep.
  • Disappearing if they don't take your advice.
